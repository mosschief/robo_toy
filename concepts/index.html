Tank, revision 3

You picked the Tank. This revision drives on a bought tank chassis instead of printed tracks, and swaps the ultrasonic sensor for a cheap Wi-Fi camera and a laser distance sensor, both in the chest. Renders are to scale (millimetres) from OpenSCAD; round 1 is further down for reference.

What the camera adds

  • Live video to a phone or tablet over Wi-Fi, with on-screen driving buttons, so your kid sees what the robot sees
  • Simple vision tricks on the ESP32: follow a coloured ball, react when a face appears
  • The distance sensor brings back "stop before the wall" and lets it measure how far away the ball is

Trade-offs

  • The camera no longer turns with the head, so to look around the robot turns its whole body
  • The chest guard takes the hits the head used to avoid, so the lens sits behind a 2 mm clear cover recessed 3 mm

How the electronics split

ESP32-CAM: eyes and Wi-Fi

Runs the camera, the phone web page and any vision code. It sends short commands like "drive forward" or "happy face" to the Nano over a serial link (two wires, plus a two-resistor divider because the ESP32 is 3.3 V).

Otto Nano: body

Keeps doing what it does in Otto: servos, the three matrices, the buzzer, and now the motor driver. It also reads the VL53L0X over I2C (pins A4 and A5), so it can stop by itself even with Wi-Fi off. Programmed in the Arduino IDE exactly as before, so it still works on its own without the camera.

Bought chassis

The tracks, sprockets, road wheels and motors come as one tested unit. The printed body bolts to its aluminium deck with four M3 screws, and printed fenders cover the tracks so fingers stay out.

Power

2 × 18650 cells (7.4 V) in the chassis feed the motors through the driver, and a 5 V 3 A buck converter feeds the Nano, servos, matrices and camera.

Pin budget fits the Nano in every concept: all three matrices share one 3-wire chain, the motor driver takes 4 PWM pins (3, 5, 6, 11), the HC-SR04 2 pins, the buzzer 1, and servos go on the remaining pins. Otto's Bluetooth module would still fit for phone control.

How it stays in one piece

Arms that give way

Each arm attaches to its servo through a magnetic or spring-detent coupler, so a yank pops the arm off instead of stripping the servo gears. Arms get metal-gear MG90S servos, which are drop-in replacements for the SG90.

Soft outside, hard inside

Tyres, tracks, bumpers and the handle are TPU. The shell is PETG with 4 walls and 30% gyroid infill, not PLA, which goes brittle and cracks on drops.

Protected screens

The matrices sit recessed behind a 2 mm polycarbonate or PETG window, so little fingers and crashes never touch the LEDs.

Floating electronics

The Nano, driver and buck converter sit on one tray on foam pads, with heat-set brass inserts instead of screws threaded into plastic, so it can be opened and fixed many times.

Kid-safe power

Lithium cells live behind a screwed battery door with a protected charger. Any magnets are fully enclosed inside printed parts; for a child under about 6 I'd use the spring detent instead of magnets.

Programmable

Standard Arduino IDE (and Otto Blockly-style blocks later), with a small library of moves, faces and sounds so new tricks are a few lines of code.
